A Region is a defined part of the world with its own character, inhabitants, dangers, and opportunities for adventure. Regions provide a common structure for describing settings, preparing sessions, and inviting players to contribute to the world their characters inhabit.

A Region might encompass a kingdom, a sprawling city, a stretch of wilderness, a network of underground settlements, or an isolated village troubled by unnatural events. Its boundaries are determined by what makes it useful in play, not by its size or geography.

Regions serve two purposes. First, they organize the setting into manageable sections that the GM can prepare and reference independently. Second, they provide a framework for connecting characters to the world and giving the party a shared stake in its places, people, and problems.

A GM running a published setting or a world of their own creation can use Regions without rebuilding the setting from scratch. Assign each Region to the appropriate part of the world, then use its contents to prepare the locations and situations the party is likely to encounter.

A Region is not an adventure or a prescribed sequence of events. It is a collection of material from which adventures can emerge.

## Using Regions

A Region is built from six parts: Identity, Hotspots, Encounters, Challenges, Finds and Points of Interest, and Questions. Each serves a distinct purpose, and together they give the GM everything needed to bring a location into play.

- Identity establishes the Region's character, ordinary life, and central tensions.
    
- Hotspots identify important places, people, and institutions where characters can form Bonds.
    
- Encounters provide immediate situations that can arise during play.
    
- Challenges present obstacles, dilemmas, and problems that require the party's attention.
    
- Finds and Points of Interest provide objects, discoveries, and locations worth exploring.
    
- Questions invite players to establish details about the Region through their characters' experiences and knowledge.


A GM does not need to prepare every part of every Region before a campaign begins. Start with the Region where the characters live or begin their adventures, then prepare additional Regions as the party approaches them. When the characters travel into unfamiliar territory, the GM can consult the appropriate Region for material suited to the environment.

Regions can also overlap in a campaign. A kingdom at war might contain an Old City Region centered on the capital and a War-Torn Villages Region covering the surrounding countryside. Each provides different material, while the conflict between them connects their events and inhabitants.

## Hotspots and Bonds

Hotspots are the significant places, communities, institutions, or individuals within a Region. They give the GM concrete locations to use during play and provide natural points of connection for the characters.

When creating a character, a player may establish a Bond with a suitable Hotspot. A Bond represents a meaningful connection to a place, family, organization, individual, or other part of the world. A character might belong to a merchant guild, serve a temple, owe their upbringing to a neighborhood, or be bound by duty to a noble household.

A Hotspot does not need to be a formal organization. A village, a family of charcoal burners, a local shrine, or an isolated monastery can all serve as a character's Bond.

The GM should provide enough information about each Hotspot for players to understand what it represents and how a character might be connected to it. Players can also propose their own Bonds, provided the GM agrees that they fit the Region and the campaign.

Bonds should give characters reasons to care about what happens in the world. They can provide access to people, places, knowledge, shelter, or other forms of assistance, while also creating obligations and potential complications. Membership or familiarity does not guarantee cooperation, and a Bond does not automatically grant a numerical bonus.

When a Region changes, its Hotspots can change with it. A guild might lose influence, a neighborhood might be devastated, or a temple might divide over a matter of faith. These developments give the GM a way to make the consequences of the party's actions visible.

## Encounters

Encounters are ready-to-use situations that can arise when the party explores a Region. They may involve creatures, inhabitants, environmental hazards, disputes, or unexpected opportunities.

An Encounter is not necessarily hostile, nor must it lead to combat. A suspicious traveler, a flooded road, a public argument, or a desperate request for assistance can all provide meaningful play.

Each Region should include a small selection of Encounters that reflect its character. The GM can choose an Encounter that suits the current circumstances or roll on a table when an unexpected development would be useful.

Encounters should provide a starting situation rather than a predetermined outcome. Describe what the characters encounter, establish what is immediately apparent, and allow their decisions to determine what follows.

## Challenges

Challenges are obstacles, dilemmas, and problems associated with a Region. They give the party reasons to investigate, negotiate, explore, take risks, or spend resources.

A Challenge might involve crossing dangerous terrain, gaining access to a restricted archive, resolving a dispute between two communities, or deciding whether to assist a group whose interests conflict with their own.

Challenges are not limited to mechanical tests. A successful Check might help a character overcome an immediate obstacle without resolving the wider problem. Gaining entry to a noble's estate, for example, does not necessarily mean convincing its owner to provide assistance.

Each Challenge should describe the problem and what makes it difficult. The GM can then determine the appropriate resolution using the existing rules and the circumstances established in play.

## Finds and Points of Interest

These two categories provide material for discovery and exploration.

Finds are objects, resources, evidence, and other things the characters might acquire or uncover. They can include mundane equipment, documents, valuable materials, unusual items, or objects that reveal something about the Region's inhabitants.

Points of Interest are locations worth noticing or exploring. They need not contain treasure or initiate a complete adventure. A neglected monument, a ruined watchtower, an unusual landmark, or a public gathering place can make a Region feel distinctive and offer opportunities for further discovery.

Both categories should be specific to the Region. A city might contain forgotten records, old seals, abandoned buildings, and hidden passages. A wilderness Region might instead offer unusual tracks, natural hazards, medicinal plants, ancient ruins, or signs of creatures living nearby.

The GM can introduce these elements when the party explores an area, investigates a lead, or needs something interesting to discover.

## Questions and Player Contributions

Questions allow players to help establish the details of the world their characters inhabit. They are short prompts designed to draw on a character's experiences, relationships, and knowledge of a Region.

When creating a Region, the GM should prepare a handful of questions that reveal its character and invite the players to contribute. During character creation or play, the GM can ask a player to answer a relevant question. Players need not answer every question, and the GM should use only those that suit the campaign.

A question might ask what a local community is known for, why people avoid a particular landmark, which family has fallen out of favor, or what tradition the character remembers from childhood.

The GM should distinguish between a character's beliefs and facts the player is establishing as true. A character might believe a forest is haunted without knowing whether it is, or might personally know that a particular noble once helped their family. Unless the question invites uncertainty, a player's contribution should generally be accepted as part of the setting.

Questions are not a requirement for elaborate improvisation. Players may answer briefly, draw on details already established, or ask the GM for clarification. Their purpose is to make the world more personal and interconnected, not to transfer the GM's entire responsibility for worldbuilding to the players.

## Guidance for the GM

A Region is a practical reference, not a checklist. Its entries exist to support play, and the GM should feel free to introduce them whenever they are relevant rather than following a prescribed order.

When preparing a campaign, begin with the Region most important to the characters. Identify a few Hotspots, establish the Region's central tension, and select enough Encounters, Challenges, Finds, and Points of Interest to support the expected sessions. Use the Questions to connect the characters to their surroundings.

As the campaign develops, expand Regions in response to the party's interests and actions. A location that becomes important can receive additional Hotspots. An unanswered question can become a new Challenge. A discovery can reveal a connection to another Region.

The goal is to make preparation modular and responsive. The GM should be able to look at a Region and quickly find something appropriate for the current situation, while the players should be able to recognize how their characters belong to the world and how their decisions can change it.
