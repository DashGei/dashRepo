Arc I: The Wall of Sleep

As a background, in ancient times Azatoth created the universe via dreaming. In this universe resides the primordials who create Gaia, and the world lives by the natural order until the gods arrive. For a short period the primordials are amendable to the gods, but as the gods begin creating arcane and divine magics, along with their own set of races beyond humans, a war breaks out and the primordials are sealed in their respective four planes.

in the year 70 P.A (Pre Atlantis), after 930 years of prehistoric civilization, the Sea Elf archmage Sorin Velthrap, while studying conjuration magic makes contact with the primordial of water Leviathan. Sorin, equivalent in impact on magic as Pythagoras to math, has become increasingly frustrated with the church’s impact on scholarly matters and the arcane arts. Leviathan tells her that with his help, they can free him and the other primordials who can banish the gods from the realms. She starts a great academy which eventually evolves into an empire in a space which is now modern day Nerelith, this nation is known as Atlantis.

Her plan here is to create enough scholars well versed in conjuration to tear a space in reality and open a gate through the prison that traps Leviathan. However this process is slow, and incredibly difficult. When a creature dies their soul moves between the planes, eventually ending up on an outer plane that aligns with their life. This creates a small hole in the fabric of space, and in one of the first use cases of the dark arts, Sorin figures if she kills enough people at once, a she can tear a gate similarly to the one that would require tens of thousands of high accomplishing wizards. She turns the entire city into a death circle and begins a ritual that would siphon all souls in the city. However the god of omens, fate, and fear Tenebris notices this, and as the ritual is completed he fouls it. This results in the city becoming stuck between the elemental plane of water and ours, and Sorin becoming the first Lich.

### Arc II Opening Scene: The Sunken Court

The fall does not end cleanly.

For a brief moment, there is no sense of direction—only the pressure of water, the disorientation of weightlessness, and the feeling of something vast moving somewhere far below. Then impact. Stone, silt, and a jarring halt that sends debris drifting upward in slow, silent clouds.

When the movement settles, the party finds themselves not in open ruin, but within a contained space.

A chamber.

At first, it appears unremarkable—another collapsed structure swallowed by time—but as vision adjusts, the shape of the room becomes clear. Tiered stone seating curves along the walls, broken in places but still deliberate in design. A raised platform stands at the far end, partially split, as though something beneath it once strained upward. The architecture is too precise to be natural, too intact to be random collapse.

This was a place built for purpose.

Light comes not from above, but from the walls themselves. A soft, bioluminescent glow seeps across the chamber in thin, veined patterns. It clings to surfaces in slow-moving layers, pooling in cracks and trailing along the edges of stone benches. The source reveals itself in fragments—shattered glass tanks, their frames rusted and broken, their contents long since spilled.

The ooze that once lived contained within them now coats the room.

It moves with a sluggish awareness, not aggressive, but not inert. Where it gathers more thickly, it pulses faintly, as though responding to something unseen. The glow is uneven, dim in some places, brighter in others, creating the impression that the room itself is breathing—light rising and falling in slow, quiet rhythms.

Sound is muted here. Even movement feels slightly delayed, as though the water carries resistance beyond the physical. Every step stirs faint currents that drift along the floor and curl toward the center of the chamber.

Carved into the stone, half-obscured by growth and sediment, are markings—clean lines and deliberate shapes that speak of structure and order. Atlantean script, formal and repeating. Symbols of authority. Judgment. Process.

This is not a temple, nor a hall of learning.

It is a court.

At some point in Atlantis’ history, when Sorin Velthrap’s academy had grown into something larger, this chamber served as a place where magic itself was put on trial. Misuse of conjuration. Forbidden research. Academic theft. Whatever power Atlantis cultivated, it was not left entirely unchecked.

The shattered tanks suggest the court once employed a method of verification—something alive, something that could respond where words could not be trusted. Now that system has spread beyond its bounds.

The ooze reacts.

Subtly at first. A shift in brightness. A faint ripple through the thin layers coating the floor. It does not respond to movement or presence alone, but to something less visible. When voices break the stillness—when statements are made with certainty or intent—the glow nearest the speaker flickers, almost imperceptibly adjusting.

And when something untrue is spoken, even slightly, the reaction is immediate.

The light sharpens. The ooze contracts and pulses, as though tasting the air. Not violently, not with attack—but with recognition.

The room remembers its purpose.

Beyond the central chamber, fractured archways lead deeper into the structure, though many are partially collapsed or obscured. One corridor slopes downward, where smoother stone begins to emerge beneath the decay—less natural cave, more deliberate construction. Elsewhere, broken doors hang ajar, their interiors dark, their contents long undisturbed.

Near the raised platform, fragments of stone tablets and sealed containers lie scattered, some cracked open, others still intact beneath layers of growth. Whatever records once defined this place have not entirely vanished.

There is no immediate threat here. No ambush waiting in the shadows.

But the silence is not empty.

It carries weight. Expectation.

And somewhere, far beyond the chamber, something ancient continues to move—unseen, but aware.
